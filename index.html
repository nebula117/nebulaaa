1Setting outPLS 210 · Presentation 1

What does working after marriage mean to rural vs. urban women in Kazakhstan?

Married women, urban and rural, working and not, describing what work means once marriage changes the terms.

Nebula
2Setting outResearch question

How do married women in Kazakhstan's urban and rural settlements describe, in 2026, the meaning of working after marriage, whether or not they currently work, and how do their accounts differ?

Population
Married women, any employment status
Context
Urban vs. rural, by KZ settlement type
Time
2026: present-day accounts
  • Rural/urban defined by Kazakhstan's own settlement classification, not one named city or village
  • Employment status left open: working and non-working women both belong here
3Why it mattersContribution

The literature explains the barriers. Nobody has asked what work means to the women inside them.

  • Structural research (childcare gaps, labor law, institutional ceilings) explains why participation lags
  • Almost nothing asks how women themselves narrate choosing to work, or not, after marriage
  • Rural vs. urban usually appears as a variable, not a real side-by-side comparison

This project puts women's own words at the center and takes the rural/urban split seriously.

4Why it mattersEpistemological positioning

Interpretivist

  • The question asks what work means, not whether a variable predicts it
  • That calls for open interviews, not a fixed hypothesis to test
  • Meaning is built in context: region, marriage, and generation shape it
  • I am part of the instrument here, not a neutral measuring tool
5The trail so farLiterature review · 1 of 2 · Structural & institutional constraints
  • Kovaleva et al. (2025)

    Traditional family structures and unpaid care work restrict women's economic participation and entrepreneurial potential in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan.

  • Buribayev & Khamzina (2019)

    Liberalization of the Labor Code reduced social and labor guarantees for women and workers with family responsibilities, worsening discrimination and gender segregation.

  • Kuzhabekova et al. (2018)

    Women leaders in the civil service face a clash between neoliberal expectations to contribute economically and traditional norms that prioritize domestic roles, hindering leadership advancement.

6The trail so farLiterature review · 2 of 2 · Care burden & ideological frame
  • Meurs et al. (2021)

    The burden of caring for very young children limits women's employment in Kazakhstan, and expanding childcare alone has not been enough to raise female employment.

  • Zabirova (2026)

    Modernization and patriarchy develop together as a "moralized patriarchy": support for women's education and public life coexists with belief in men's economic priority, and hierarchy is re-legitimized as duty and virtue.

The gap: all five describe the pattern from the outside. None ask women what working after marriage means to them, or compare rural and urban accounts.

Four sensitizing concepts, read through one frame

Moralized patriarchy: the frame that holds the other three in place
Economic necessitywhat can make work necessary
Identity-driven workwhat can make work meaningful
Family / in-law pressurewhat can make work costly
  • Sensitizing concepts tell me where to listen in interviews; they are not hypotheses to test
  • Each one comes from the reviewed literature. The next slides show the evidence and what I will listen for
  • I stay open to concepts the interviews raise that are not on this list
8Finding the lensAnalytical framework · Concept 1 of 4
Economic necessity

Work as a household need

What the literature shows

  • Kuzhabekova et al. 2018 Women leaders in the civil service are pressed to earn while still held to traditional family-role expectations
  • Kuzhabekova et al. 2018 The pressure is framed as a clash between neoliberal values (contribute, achieve) and traditional expectations of women
  • Zabirova 2026 Survey evidence (N = 8,101) shows support for women's public participation alongside belief in men's economic priority, so need-driven work may not change who counts as the main earner

What I will listen for

  • Is work explained as "we need the income," and does that make it easier to justify to family?
  • Do rural and urban women weigh need and choice differently?
  • The case that sparked this question: a rural accountant who works because one wage is not enough
9Finding the lensAnalytical framework · Concept 2 of 4
Identity-driven work

Work as part of who she is

What the literature shows

  • Kuzhabekova et al. 2018 Women leaders must reconcile professional roles with traditional expectations of them as wives and mothers
  • Zabirova 2026 Higher education and urban residence go with more egalitarian views, the conditions where work could be valued beyond household duty
  • Least directly documented of the four, so it is a lens to listen for, not a finding

What I will listen for

  • Is work described as self-worth, ambition, or independence, or only as a means?
  • Does continuing to work feel like a right, a negotiation, or a favor granted?
  • My expectation: for urban women, marriage may limit the identity-driven jobs they want
10Finding the lensAnalytical framework · Concept 3 of 4
Family / in-law pressure

Work negotiated inside the family

What the literature shows

  • Kovaleva et al. 2025 Traditional family structures and unpaid care duties restrict women's economic participation
  • Kuzhabekova et al. 2018 Traditional family-role expectations shape how women leaders experience their work
  • Buribayev & Khamzina 2019 At work, expectations of women and men are the same; in the family, the stereotype of male privilege persists
  • Zabirova 2026 Traditional views are more common among rural residents and older respondents

What I will listen for

  • Who has a say (husband, parents-in-law, wider family), and how is it voiced?
  • Is the pressure explicit, or carried as an expectation of what a wife does?
  • My expectation: rural women's work is judged more through a traditional in-law lens
11Finding the lensAnalytical framework · Concept 4 of 4
Moralized patriarchy

The frame: hierarchy held in place by duty and virtue

What the literature shows

  • Zabirova 2026 A nationally representative survey (N = 8,101) finds a hybrid regime: support for women's education and public participation alongside men's economic priority and authority
  • Zabirova 2026 Hierarchy persists through duty, virtue, and family harmony; the "moralized patriarchal compromise" is the mechanism
  • Zabirova 2026 Traditional views are more common among men, older respondents, rural residents, highly religious people, and in the south and west; higher education and urban living go with more egalitarian views
  • Kuzhabekova et al. 2018 The same clash of values appears in women leaders' own accounts

What I will listen for

  • Is working after marriage framed as duty to the family, as virtue, or as her own right?
  • Are there conditions attached, such as "as long as the home is looked after"?
  • Does the compromise sound different in rural and urban accounts?
12Finding the lensWorking expectations

What I expect going in, and could be wrong about

Urban women

  • Tension around pursuing identity-driven jobs
  • Better infrastructure supporting that work

Rural women

  • Weaker infrastructure; employment opportunity skews toward men
  • Women's work judged through a more traditional in-law lens

Expectations, not conclusions. The interviews get the final say.

15ArrivalReflection

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don't

  • Scoping: a broad question needs an explicit population, setting, and time frame before it can be answered
  • Interpretivist logic: a question about meaning calls for open interviews and sensitizing concepts, not testable hypotheses
  • Reading sources: separating what each study establishes from what I had assumed it showed
  • Still unresolved: how I will recruit participants, especially in rural settlements
  • Still unresolved: whether these are the right four concepts, particularly identity-driven work, which the literature documents least directly
  • Still unresolved: how my urban positionality shapes what I notice in rural interviews
